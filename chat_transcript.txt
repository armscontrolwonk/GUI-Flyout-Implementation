The code computes exactly one heating quantity: the **Sutton-Graves stagnation-point heat flux** (line 2625), using a conventional 5 cm placeholder nose radius. That's it — the gap between that and "temperature of the RV" is substantial.

**What Sutton-Graves gives you:**

```
q̇_stag = 1.7415×10⁻⁴ · √(ρ / R_N) · v³     [W/m²]
```

This is the flux at the single point of highest compression on the nose tip. It scales with airspeed cubed and density, and inversely with √R_N. The code reports the peak value during the glide phase as a milestone event ("Peak heating X MW/m²"). It does not integrate total heat load and does not attempt a temperature.

**The three layers of missing physics:**

**1. Flux → stagnation temperature** requires a thermal boundary condition:
- *Radiation equilibrium* (simplest): `T_stag = (q̇ / σε)^(1/4)`. At 30 MW/m² and ε = 0.9 this is ~5,000 K — beyond any structural material, so real HGVs use ablative TPS or ceramic composites
- *Ablative*: surface stays near ablation temperature (~3,500 K for carbon-carbon); heat load `Q = ∫q̇ dt` determines ablator consumed, not peak flux
- *Heat-sink*: `ΔT = Q / (m · cₚ)` for the structure

**2. Distribution around the body** — stagnation is the maximum; elsewhere it falls off. A Lees-type laminar correlation gives the shape: `q̇(θ)/q̇_stag ≈ f(θ, cone half-angle, boundary layer state)`. For a typical ICBM cone (half-angle 10–15°), the conical surface flux is roughly 10–30% of stagnation, depending on angle of attack and local Mach. The windward side at nonzero AoA runs 2–3× the leeward side. The base is essentially zero (wake). Those distributions require CFD or semi-empirical correlations for the specific geometry.

**3. Internal temperature** is yet another layer — the thermal conductivity of the TPS material and the standoff insulation between it and the payload. That's a 1D or 3D heat conduction problem in the RV structure.

**What the 5 cm R_N convention means:** The peak *time* (when the event is logged) is independent of nose radius — it's set by where ρ·v³ peaks on the trajectory. Only the reported flux magnitude scales with R_N. So the milestone tells you "aerothermal severity peaked here on the trajectory" reliably, but the MW/m² number is only meaningful relative to actual R_N.

**Bottom line for the codebase:** The code's single heating output is the right figure of merit for *trajectory-level* aerothermal severity assessment — it's what preliminary design tools use to compare trajectories. To get to actual RV temperatures you'd need the RV's actual nose radius, its TPS material properties, and either a Lees distribution or CFD — none of which belong in a trajectory integrator.

No. The RV schema stores `diameter_m` and `length_m`, but `beta_kg_m2` is supplied as a **direct input** — it's not derived from the geometry. And crucially, the Sutton-Graves nose radius `R_N` is the **radius of curvature at the nose tip** (the spherical cap), which is a separate geometric parameter from the body diameter. For a cone, those two are completely independent.

The `diameter_m` tells you the base cross-section (which would let you back out Cd if you knew mass, but that's circular since beta already encodes m/(Cd·A)). Yes — those are the next two layers, and they buy you progressively more but also progressively more belong outside a trajectory tool.

**Lees distribution (or simpler Kemp-Riddell)** — axisymmetric correlation that maps `q̇_stag` to `q̇(θ)` along the body. For a sphere-cone it's basically:
- Stagnation region: full `q̇_stag`
- Cone flank: ~10–30% of stagnation (drops with half-angle, recovers slightly at high Mach)
- Base: ~0

This lets you compute total heat *absorbed* (flank area × flank flux, integrated over time), which matters more than peak flux for ablator sizing. It's a closed-form correlation, ~20 lines of code, and could reasonably live in the trajectory tool. Limits: axisymmetric (no AoA asymmetry — windward/leeward at α requires the Eckert reference-enthalpy method or CFD).

**TPS properties** — at least three numbers per material:
- Emissivity ε (for radiative equilibrium: `T_surface = (q̇/σε)^(1/4)` — one line)
- Ablation enthalpy h_w (for recession rate: `ṡ ≈ q̇/(ρ·h_w)`)
- Effective heat capacity `ρ·cp·k` (for transient conduction)

Equilibrium surface temperature is trivial and could be a free addition. **Actual** RV temperatures — back-face, payload, time-dependent through-thickness — need 1D heat conduction through the TPS. That's a transient PDE, and it's exactly what dedicated tools like FIAT, MINIVER, or CMA exist to do. They take `q̇_stag(t)` from a trajectory tool as input and produce the thermal solution.

**Reasonable stopping point for this tool:** peak stagnation flux (done) + total integrated heat load (trivial: `np.trapz(q_dot, t)`) + radiative-equilibrium stagnation temperature (one line, with a default ε ≈ 0.85). That covers the standard preliminary-design figures of merit. Anything beyond — Lees distribution across the body, ablator recession, back-face temperatures — is a thermal-protection sizing problem that wants its own tool.

The two models differ in how they compute lift and drag during off-trim maneuvers. **At trim they're identical** — the difference shows up only when the vehicle is pulling Gs or hard-banking.

**`constant_LD`** (default) — the simple model
- Drag: `D = q·m/β` (β-based, decoupled from lift)
- Lift: `L = D·(L/D)` at all times (or trimmed to `m·g⊥/cos σ` in equilibrium-glide mode)
- Implicit assumption: vehicle is always at the trim AoA where L/D is maximum
- L/D is a constant input that doesn't change with AoA
- **Matches the closed-form Tracy/Acton analytical equilibrium-glide solution exactly**

**`polar`** (slender-body) — the physics-based model
- Drag polar: `C_D = C_D0 + k·C_L²`
- Lift coefficient: `C_L = 2·α` (Munk 1924 / Ashley & Landahl §6-7, §9-8 slender-body theory)
- `C_D0` and `k` are back-solved from the user's β and (L/D)_max so:
  - At zero lift: drag matches what β predicts (`C_D0 = m / (β·A_ref)`)
  - At trim α*: L/D exactly matches the user's input (`k = 1/(4·C_D0·(L/D)_max²)`)
- AoA limit: |α| ≤ 25° (slender-body theory validity boundary)
- Reference area: `A_ref = π·d²/4` (base area)

**Where they diverge:**

| Scenario | constant_LD | polar |
|---|---|---|
| **Steady glide at trim** | Same | Same |
| **Pull-up (N·g maneuver)** | Drag unchanged; L just increases | C_L must rise → C_D rises quadratically → real energy loss |
| **Hard bank to maintain altitude** | cos σ factor on vertical L; drag unchanged | Need more total L → more C_L → more C_D → more drag |
| **Post-burnout coast above 86 km** | Lift fully available | C_D ≈ 2·C_D0 at α* → costs velocity during ascent through 86–120 km, drops apogee |
| **Skip-glide phugoid** | Lift = D·(L/D) follows drag amplitude | Trims at α*, so L ∝ q and the natural phugoid is preserved |

**Practical guidance:**

- **Use `constant_LD`** when comparing to analytical Tracy/Acton equilibrium-glide solutions, or for trajectories dominated by trim flight. It's computationally simpler and matches closed-form benchmarks exactly. Good default.
- **Use `polar`** when the trajectory has significant maneuvering (hard banks, pull-ups, dive recoveries) and you care about the realistic drag penalty for off-trim flight. The phugoid behavior is also more physical — lift naturally tracks dynamic pressure rather than being bolted to drag.

**For C-HGB specifically**, with L/D = 2.0 and the planned skip-to-equilibrium guidance:
- During the skip phase, `polar` will preserve the phugoid more realistically (lift ∝ q during the oscillation)
- After handoff to equilibrium glide, both models trim toward the same closed-form solution
- The differences matter most if you add bank schedules or aggressive maneuvers later

If you just want the trajectory to "look right" and match Tracy/Acton textbook predictions, `constant_LD` is fine. If you want energy accounting during maneuvers to be physically defensible, `polar`.
